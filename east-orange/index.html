Keeping Your East Orange Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year if their water is hard.

The age of your heater plays a big role. With regular maintenance, a standard tank usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like rust-colored water, popping noises, or a damp spot under the tank, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule is cheaper and is far less stressful than an emergency swap with water spreading across the floor.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip ignored for months can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Typical Water Heater Issues in East Orange

The majority of water heater failures we see in East Orange come back to what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Age matters just as much. Plenty of homes around here still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and beyond the ten-year mark, the anode rod is usually spent and rust begins attacking the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, including heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
